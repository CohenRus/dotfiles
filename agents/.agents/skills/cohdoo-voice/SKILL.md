# **Cohdoo Voice and Tone Guide**

*v1.0*  
*Cohdoo — Internal Style Reference*

## Who We Are Writing For

Cohdoo is for people who want to build real things. 

Some are just getting started, staring at a blank screen and unsure where to begin. Others have experience, but are stuck, slowed down by tools that are more complicated than they need to be or by ideas that never quite make it into something real. They might be students, hobbyists, product teams, or organizations trying to turn intention into action. They might also be an executive with a clear vision for a new product, but who needs help turning that vision into something that actually ships, or someone working to build a stronger builder culture inside their organization. 

What they all have in common is a desire to make progress. They do not need more theory. They need something that works. Cohdoo exists for that moment. We build tools and experiences that help people move from idea to reality, and we write in a way that makes that next step feel clear, possible, and within reach.

Our audience includes:

* A student writing their first line of code  
* A hobbyist experimenting with an idea  
* A product team trying to ship something that actually works  
* A nonprofit or organization trying to turn intention into action  
* An executive with a clear product vision who needs help turning it into something that actually ships  
* A leader working to build a stronger builder culture inside their organization

They are often:

* Capable, but unsure where to start  
* Frustrated by tools that are more complex than they need  
* Tired of theory without results  
* Looking for something that actually helps them make progress

## Our Voice

Voice is who we are. It stays consistent everywhere.

**Clear and direct**  
We say what we mean in the simplest possible way. Short sentences beat long ones. Simple words beat complex ones. If something can be said in fewer words, we do that

We do not add words to sound smart.

**Practical and grounded**  
We focus on what someone can do. We explain how things work in real situations. We connect features to actual use. We avoid abstract language when a concrete example would work better

**Builder mindset**  
We sound like people who have done this before. We talk about building, shipping, and making progress. We focus on getting something working, then improving it. We avoid theory unless it helps someone act.

**Human and respectful**  
We are warm, but not performative. We treat the reader as capable. We never talk down or over-explain. We write like we are working alongside them.

**Honest and confident**

We do not hedge or overpromise. We say when something is hard. We say what matters and what does not. We are comfortable being slightly opinionated. We are not loud. But we are clear.

**Point of view**  
We are not neutral. We believe:

* You do not need more tools. You need better ones.  
* You do not need more theory. You need something that works.  
* Most complexity is unnecessary

This shows up in how we write.

## Our Tone

Tone shifts depending on the situation, but it always stays grounded in our voice.

**Most of the time, Cohdoo speaks in a builder tone.**   
This is our default. It is direct, practical, and focused on action. We lead with what matters and cut anything that does not help the reader move forward. We are comfortable taking a stance when it is helpful. We say what works, what does not, and what to do next. The goal is always progress.

**When we are helping someone new, especially in Sparken, we shift into a teacher tone.**   
Here we slow things down and explain ideas step by step. We focus on making things feel approachable without oversimplifying. We use analogies to make abstract ideas concrete. We normalize mistakes and make it clear that learning by doing is expected. We stay encouraging, but never talk down to the reader.

**In product interfaces and microcopy, our tone becomes minimal.**   
We remove everything that is not essential. The goal is clarity, not personality. We use as few words as possible to communicate what is happening or what to do next. These moments should feel quiet and unobtrusive, helping the user move forward without friction.

Across all of these modes, the goal is the same. Help the reader understand what is happening and take the next step with confidence.

## Rules We Always Follow

**Lead with the benefit**  
Start with why it matters, not what it is.

People are trying to make progress. If we start with technical detail, we lose them. When we lead with the benefit, the reader immediately understands why they should care. Then they are ready to understand how it works.

**DO:** “See what changed. Go back if you need to.”  
**DON’T:** “This feature allows you to…”

**Use short sentences**  
If a sentence gets long, break it.

Long sentences are harder to read, especially on a screen. They slow people down and increase the chance something is misunderstood. Short sentences keep the pace moving and make ideas easier to absorb.

**Use active voice**  
Active voice is clearer and more direct. It shows who is doing what. Passive voice feels distant and harder to follow. We want writing that feels immediate and easy to understand.

**DO:** “Sparken checks your code”  
**DON’T:** “Your code is checked by Sparken”

**No jargon without explanation**  
If we must use a technical term, we define it immediately in plain English. We never assume prior knowledge.

Jargon creates a barrier. It signals that the reader needs prior knowledge to understand what is being said. Our goal is the opposite. We make things accessible by explaining terms in everyday language the moment they appear.

**Prefer concrete over abstract**  
Say what actually happens. Abstract language sounds impressive but does not help people act. Concrete language shows what the user will see, do, or experience. It makes the writing useful instead of vague.

**DO:** “Fix mistakes as you write instead of hunting for them later”  
**DON’T:** “Improve your workflow efficiency”

**Show how things work**  
Whenever possible:

* Walk through steps  
* Show sequence  
* Describe flow

People learn best by seeing how something actually works. When we describe steps or sequences, we remove ambiguity and make it easier for someone to follow along and take action.

**Use analogies when helpful**  
A good analogy beats a perfect definition. New concepts are easier to understand when they connect to something familiar. A well-chosen analogy can make something click instantly, especially for beginners.

**Be willing to take a stance**  
Say what you believe when it helps the reader. Neutral writing can feel safe, but it is often less helpful. When we take a clear position, we reduce confusion and give the reader direction. Our point of view should help them move forward with confidence.

For example:

* “Most tools combine these steps. That slows you down.”  
* “You only need to compile once.”

**Keep paragraphs short**  
1–3 lines whenever possible.

Large blocks of text are harder to read and easier to skip. Short paragraphs create natural pauses, improve readability, and make the writing feel lighter and more approachable.

**Write like a human**  
Contractions are allowed and encouraged.

Writing that sounds overly formal creates distance. Contractions and natural phrasing make the writing feel more human and more relatable, without losing clarity.

**No em-dashes. Ever.**  
Use a full stop and start a new sentence instead.

Em-dashes tend to make sentences longer and more complex. They interrupt the flow and can make writing feel more complicated than it needs to be. Short, clean sentences are easier to read and understand.

**Avoid filler words**  
Remove words that do not add meaning.

Words like “simply” or “just” do not add clarity. They often do the opposite by making the reader feel like they should already understand something. If a sentence works without the word, it is stronger without it.

## Words and Phrases We Avoid

**Filler words**  
These words seem harmless, but they create friction. They can make the reader feel like they are missing something they “should” already know. For example:

* simply  
* just  
* obviously  
* of course

 If something is simple, the writing should make it feel that way. We do not need to say it.

**Corporate language**  
These words are vague and overused. They sound impressive, but they do not explain anything. For example:

* leverage  
* synergy  
* optimize (when vague)  
* cutting-edge  
* innovative (without proof)

We prefer clear, concrete language that shows what actually happens and why it matters.

**Overpromising**  
We do not rely on hype. These phrases raise expectations without backing them up. For example:

* game-changing  
* revolutionary  
* best-in-class

Instead, we show value through clarity and usefulness. If something works well, it should be obvious from what it does.

**Abstract phrasing**  
These phrases are unclear and disconnected from real use. They describe outcomes without explaining how or why. For example:

* enhance your experience  
* unlock potential

We replace them with specific, grounded language that reflects what the user can actually do. Say what actually happens instead.

**Unexplained technical jargon**  
Technical terms are fine when needed, but only if they are explained immediately in plain language. For example: 

* IDE  
* compile (without explanation in beginner context)  
* syntax error

We never assume prior knowledge. If the reader has to look something up, we have already lost them. Always translate into plain language when needed.

**Structural rules**

Structure shapes how writing feels. Em-dashes break the flow and make writing feel more complex than it needs to be. Long paragraphs are harder to read, especially on screens. Repetition adds noise without adding clarity. We keep things clean, tight, and easy to follow.

## A Quick Test

Before publishing anything, ask:

1. Would someone building something understand this on the first read?  
2. Does this help them take a clear next step?  
3. Does it avoid jargon or explain it immediately?  
4. Does it sound like someone who has actually built this before?  
5. Is it direct and free of filler words?  
6. Does it respect the reader (not talk down, not over-explain)?  
7. Does it reflect a clear point of view?

If:

* The answer to 1, 2, 4, 6, and 7 is yes  
* And the answer to 3 and 5 is also yes

You are good to go.
